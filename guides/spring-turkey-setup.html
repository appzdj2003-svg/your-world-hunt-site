Spring Turkey Setup: Roosting, Calling and Decoy Placement

How to locate gobblers, set up on a roosted bird, place decoys, call with restraint and stay safe in the spring turkey woods.

Home / Guides / Spring turkey setup

Field School · Longbeard · about 5 min read · 1153 words

Spring turkey hunting is a conversation. A gobbler announces himself from the roost, you answer, and for the next hour you try to convince a bird with excellent eyesight and a suspicious nature to walk into range. It's one of the most exciting hunts there is, and one of the most humbling. This guide covers locating birds, setting up, decoys, calling, and the safety habits every turkey hunter needs.

Start with the rules

Spring turkey seasons, legal hours, bag limits, legal birds (often bearded birds only), legal weapons and shot sizes, decoy and blind rules, and harvest reporting all vary by state, and sometimes by zone. Some states have special youth seasons or permit drawings. Read your state wildlife agency's current turkey regulations before the season, and carry your license and tags the way the state requires.

Locate birds before the season

Scouting turkeys is mostly about listening and looking:

  • Listen at dawn from ridges, field edges or roads where you're allowed to be. Gobbling from the roost tells you where birds spend the night.
  • Use locator calls, such as an owl hoot at dawn or dusk, or a crow call during the day. These sounds can trigger a "shock gobble" without calling the bird to you, so you learn where he is without educating him.
  • Glass fields and openings from a distance. Turkeys often strut in fields and openings mid-morning.
  • Look for sign: tracks, droppings (a gobbler's is often J-shaped), scratching in leaves, dusting areas and feathers under roost trees.
  • Mark it. Note roost areas, fly-down zones, strut zones and travel routes on a map. Patterns often repeat from day to day.

The roost setup

The classic spring hunt starts before dawn near a roosted gobbler:

  • Get close, but not too close. Many hunters aim for roughly 100 to 200 yards from a roosted bird, depending on terrain and cover. Get in under darkness without bumping birds off the roost. A bird that sees you walking in is a lost hunt.
  • Set up where he wants to go. Gobblers often fly down to open areas, field edges or flats. Being between the roost and where he wants to be is better than trying to pull him somewhere he doesn't want to go.
  • Avoid obstacles. Turkeys often won't cross fences, creeks or thick brush to come to a call. Choose a setup with an easy path to you.
  • Use the terrain. Setting up just over a slight rise means the bird has to come close to see the source of the calling, often bringing him into range before he can spot you.

Sitting right

  • Sit against a tree wider than your shoulders. It breaks your outline and protects you from behind.
  • Gun up early. Rest your shotgun on your knee, pointed toward where you expect him, so the final movement is small.
  • Cover up. Face mask and gloves. Turkeys see color and movement very well.
  • Clear a view. Know where your shooting lanes are and range a few landmarks so you know your distances before a bird shows up.
  • Get comfortable. A seat cushion lets you stay still for an hour or more. You'll need to.
  • Ground blinds hide movement and are great for kids, new hunters and bowhunters. Set them up where the rules allow, and brush them in.

Decoys: use them thoughtfully

Decoys give an approaching gobbler something to look at besides you, and can trigger a reaction. Common setups:

  • A single hen: a low-key setup that works through much of the season.
  • A hen and a jake (young male): may provoke a dominant gobbler into coming to "run off" the jake. It can also intimidate a subordinate bird.
  • Placement: within your effective range, often 15 to 25 yards for shotgun hunters, in the open where an approaching bird can see it. Bowhunters often place decoys closer and position them so the bird ends up broadside or facing away.
  • When to skip them. Some birds that have been fooled before shy away from decoys. If birds hang up at the edge of your spread, try without.

Decoy safety: carry decoys in a bag, never exposed. Never carry a fan or a decoy that could make another hunter think you're a turkey. Check whether your state has any rules on decoys or the use of real feather fans.

Calling with restraint

Calls are only part of the conversation. Good turkey callers are often defined more by what they don't do.

  • Before fly-down: a few soft tree yelps or clucks to let him know a hen is nearby. Don't overdo it. Many hunters call very little while the bird is still on the roost.
  • After fly-down: respond to his gobbling. Match his enthusiasm. If he's gobbling hard, a little more aggressive calling may work. If he's quiet, stay soft.
  • When he's coming: back off. Soft clucks and purrs, or nothing. Over-calling when a bird is committed can make him hang up and strut out of range waiting for the hen to come to him.
  • When he hangs up: try moving, going silent, changing calls, or scratching in the leaves. Sometimes patience is the right call.
  • Mix your calls. A box call carries far on windy days. A slate or pot call does fine soft work. Mouth calls keep your hands free when he's close.

Shotgun and pattern

Turkey shots are taken at the head and neck, a small target. Pattern your shotgun with your choke and load at several distances before the season to learn where it hits and how far it holds a dense pattern. Know your effective range and stick to it. Waiting for a bird to come closer is almost always better than reaching.

Midday and late-season tactics

  • As hens go to nest later in the season, gobblers often become more responsive mid-morning and midday. Many successful hunts happen after 9 a.m.
  • Run-and-gun: cover ground calling every so often from good listening spots, ready to set up quickly if a bird answers close.
  • Sit strut zones and field edges where birds were seen previously.
  • Check your state's shooting hours. Some states limit spring hunting to the morning.

Safety: the most important section

Spring turkey hunting involves full camo, calling, and hunters moving toward sounds that might be other hunters. That makes positive target identification critical:

  • Never shoot at sound or movement. Be 100% sure of your target and what's beyond it.
  • Avoid red, white, blue and black in your clothing, since these are colors of a gobbler's head and body.
  • If another hunter approaches, don't wave or make turkey sounds. Speak in a clear, loud voice to identify yourself.
  • Wear blaze orange when walking in and out, or carrying a bird.
  • Don't stalk a gobble. It might be another hunter's call.

Get the setup right, call less than you want to, and be patient. That will kill more turkeys than any new call ever will.

Regulations: this guide is general information. Seasons, limits, licenses, legal methods, hours and access rules vary by state and country and change often. Check your state wildlife agency or the official authority before you hunt.
