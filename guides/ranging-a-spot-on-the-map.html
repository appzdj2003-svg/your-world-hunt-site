Ranging a Spot on the Map: GPS Distance and Known-Size Ranging

How to get the distance to a bait barrel, tree or opening with your phone: GPS distance to a marked spot, known-size camera ranging, GPS error at bow range, and low-light technique.

English·Español

Field School · Spot On · about 7 min read · 1511 words

Many shots aren't at an animal standing broadside in the open. A bear comes to a bait barrel, a buck steps into an opening, a turkey walks past a lone tree. Knowing those distances before the animal shows up is the calm way to hunt: you range the spot once, in good light, and then you already know the answer. This guide covers two ways to get a distance with a phone, what each one can and can't do, and how to use them together. Both give estimates, not laser readings, and both are best confirmed with a laser rangefinder.

Two ways to measure: GPS distance and known-size ranging

  • GPS distance works out the distance between two points on the map: where your phone is and a spot you've marked. It doesn't need to see the target at all, so it works on a barrel, a tree or an opening. Its accuracy depends on the phone's GPS fix.
  • Known-size (camera) ranging compares how big something looks in the camera with how big it really is. It needs a target of known size in view, and its accuracy depends on how well that size matches and how carefully you line it up.

Neither one is a laser. Think of them as a way to get close, then confirm with a laser rangefinder or with landmarks you've ranged before.

GPS distance to a marked spot

In Your World Hunt you can measure from where you are to any mark on the map:

  1. Drop a mark on the spot. Tap MARK, choose a mark type, then tap the map where the barrel, tree or opening is. The most accurate way is to walk to the spot and tap your own position on the map.
  2. Go to your stand or blind.
  3. Select the mark. Open your marks list and tap it. The Return card at the bottom of the map shows the mark's name, its distance from you and the compass bearing to it, with an arrow pointing the way.

No mark there yet? Long-press any open spot on the map. The MAP RANGE card shows the GPS distance from you to that spot, a ± figure based on your current GPS accuracy, and the bearing. At bow range it reminds you to confirm with the camera or a laser.

Yards or meters. Yards is the default: distances under about 175 yards show in yards, and farther ones in miles. Prefer meters? Choose METERS under UNITS in Settings, or, in the latest version, tap YD or M at the top of the camera rangefinder. It's one setting, so the map and the rangefinder always match.

The GPS chip on the map shows your phone's current accuracy (for example, "GPS ±4m"). Check it before you trust a number.

Why GPS error matters at bow range

Phone GPS is good, but it isn't exact. GPS.gov notes that GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within about 16 feet (4.9 meters) under open sky, and that accuracy gets worse near trees and buildings. A GPS distance uses two positions, the mark and you, and each one can be off. Under a canopy, the result can easily be off by several yards.

  • At rifle distances, a few yards of error rarely changes your hold.
  • At bow distances, it matters a lot. If a bait barrel is really 18 yards away and the GPS says 24, that's the difference between a good pin and a miss or a bad hit.

Ways to tighten it up: place marks with the phone held still under the most open sky you can find, wait a few seconds for the accuracy number to settle, and check the distance from your stand on more than one day. Better still, pace it off or confirm it with a laser rangefinder once, then write the number down.

Known-size camera ranging

The camera rangefinder in Your World Hunt uses known-size math. You line up two brackets on the top and bottom (or left and right edges) of a target, and the app turns the target's apparent size into a distance. It runs on the phone with a live camera preview only; no picture is saved or uploaded. It's part of the Sharp Shooter Pack, a separate add-on that isn't included in Premium. In the latest version, tap the ◎ RANGE button on the right side of the map and the camera rangefinder opens in one tap. (If you don't have the pack yet, RANGE takes you to PACKS instead.) It's also still under SHARP on the weather and wind strip, next to the Wind Hold guide.

  • Pick the right preset. Animal presets (Whitetail, Elk, Turkey, Hog, Coyote, Black bear) use typical adult sizes. Everyday presets (Person, Pickup truck, Fence post, Door, Stop sign, Bait barrel) let you range objects of known size. Real animals vary by 10 to 15 percent or more, and that error carries straight into the distance.
  • Use Custom for anything you can measure. Enter the real height or width and switch between height and width.
  • Try AI fit. It looks for an animal or object in the frame and snaps the brackets to it. You can tap an object to lock onto it, then nudge the lines to fit. It works best when the target fills a decent part of the frame; when the target is small, the readout adds "zoom in". Use + and − or pinch to zoom.
  • Read the whole readout. The number comes with "Estimate, not a laser" and a ± percentage. A wide band means a rough answer.
  • Calibrate once. Tap CALIBRATE, bracket an object of known size at a known distance (for example, a 1-yard stick at 20 yards), and tap SET. If the readout shows CALIBRATE, the phone's camera numbers weren't available and calibration matters even more.

Why aiming at a barrel, tree or opening fails without a known size

Known-size ranging only works when the app knows how big the thing in the brackets really is. Bracket a tree trunk, an opening in the brush or a patch of ground with an animal preset selected, and the app will still do the math as if it were a deer. The number looks precise, but it means nothing. So:

  • Bait barrels: a standard 55-gallon steel drum is roughly 34 to 35 inches tall and about 23 inches across. Pick the Bait barrel preset, which uses a standard 55-gallon drum, or measure yours and enter it in Custom if it's different. Then bracket the barrel.
  • Trees and openings: they have no standard size. Use a GPS mark instead, or range a known-size object you place there, like a fence post or a stick you've measured.
  • Bears and other animals: there's a Black bear preset, but bears vary a lot in size, and so do animals without a preset. The more reliable number usually comes from ranging the barrel or a marked tree next to where the animal will stand.

When to use each, and how they pair

  • Before the sit (best): mark the bait barrel, the opening and a couple of trees, then read their distances from your stand. Check them with the camera on a known-size object, or with a laser, and write the numbers down.
  • From the stand, target in view: camera ranging on the animal or on a known-size object beside it.
  • Target out of sight or too small for the camera: GPS distance to a mark, with its error in mind.
  • When the two disagree: trust the one you've confirmed. If you haven't confirmed either, treat the distance as unknown and don't take a shot that depends on it.

Once you have a distance, USE IN WIND HOLD sends it to the Wind Hold guide.

Low-light technique

Bears, deer and hogs often show up at last light, exactly when cameras struggle. Plan for it:

  • Range in daylight. Mark and range your landmarks while it's light. At dusk, use the distances you already know instead of ranging a dark shape.
  • Pick high-contrast references. A light-colored barrel, a pale stump or a fence post is easier to bracket in dim light than a dark animal against dark timber.
  • Brace the phone. Rest it against the tree, a shooting rail or your knee. Zooming in magnifies shake.
  • Check the band. If the ± figure gets wide or the brackets won't settle, stop trusting the number.
  • Respect legal light. Legal shooting hours are set by your state. A distance you can measure isn't a shot you can legally take.

Quick checklist

  • Barrel, opening and key trees marked, ideally from the spot itself
  • Distances read from the stand and confirmed once with a laser or by pacing
  • Camera rangefinder calibrated; Bait barrel preset or a Custom size set for your barrel
  • Units set to yards or meters, whichever you think in
  • GPS accuracy checked before trusting a GPS distance, especially at bow range
  • Numbers written down before dark
