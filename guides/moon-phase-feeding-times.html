Moon Phase and Feeding Times: How to Use Them (Honestly)

What solunar tables and moon phases can and can't tell you about deer movement, and how to fit them into a hunting plan alongside weather, wind and the rut.

Home / Guides / Moon phase & feeding times

Field School · Night Sky · about 4 min read · 978 words

Few hunting topics start more campfire arguments than the moon. Some hunters plan their whole vacation around moon phase. Others think it's folklore. The truth, as best anyone can tell, is somewhere in between: the moon may influence deer activity a little, but weather, food, the rut and hunting pressure usually matter much more. This guide explains what solunar tables are, what research has and hasn't shown, and how to use moon information sensibly in a hunting plan.

What "feeding times" and solunar tables are

Solunar tables come from a simple idea: animal activity may peak at certain positions of the moon. A typical table lists:

  • Major periods: roughly when the moon is overhead (transit) or directly underfoot. Often shown as windows of about two hours.
  • Minor periods: around moonrise and moonset. Usually shorter windows.
  • Day ratings: often higher around the new and full moon, and sometimes boosted when a major or minor period overlaps sunrise or sunset.

These times are calculated from astronomy, so they're precise about where the moon is. Whether deer follow them is a separate question.

What the evidence says (and doesn't)

Research on deer and the moon has produced mixed results. Some studies tracking GPS-collared deer have found modest changes in movement associated with moon phase or moon position. Others have found little or no consistent effect. Results differ between regions, seasons, individual deer and the way the studies were designed. Some biologists point out that whitetail breeding timing in much of their range is mainly driven by photoperiod (day length), not the moon.

The fair summary: there isn't strong, consistent evidence that moon phase or solunar periods reliably predict when deer will move. If there's an effect, it's probably small next to:

  • Weather. Temperature swings, fronts, wind and precipitation.
  • Food. Acorn crops, crop harvest and what's available where.
  • The rut. Breeding behavior can override almost everything for bucks.
  • Hunting pressure. Pressured deer get more nocturnal regardless of the moon.
  • Daylight. Deer are typically most active around dawn and dusk (crepuscular).

So moon data is not useless, but it's the weakest of your inputs. Treat any app, table or chart that promises certainty with skepticism, including ours.

Why hunters still find moon data useful

Even if the effect on deer is small, there are good practical reasons to keep the moon in your plan:

  • Light. A bright full moon lights your walk in and out and changes how visible you are on open ground. A new moon means a darker approach.
  • Moonrise and moonset times tell you when that light will be there, useful for planning entry and exit, and for night-time travel to a stand.
  • Legal shooting hours are defined by the sun, not the moon, in most places. Check your state's rules. But knowing sunrise and sunset times precisely helps you be in position on time.
  • A tiebreaker. If you have several equally good days on the weather, wind and rut calendar, picking the one with a midday moon transit doesn't hurt. Some hunters like it for late-morning sits.
  • Your own records. Logging moon phase alongside what you see over several seasons can show whether it seems to matter on your property.

How to fit the moon into a hunting plan

Here's a sensible order of priorities for choosing which days and sits to hunt:

  1. Season and regulations. What's legal, when, and where? Confirm with your state agency.
  2. Rut timing for your area, if you're hunting whitetail in the fall. Local biologists or state agency reports often describe typical timing.
  3. Weather. Cold fronts, temperature drops, the day after a storm, and steady winds you can hunt are all strong reasons to pick a day.
  4. Wind for your stands. Only hunt stands that fit the wind (see playing the wind).
  5. Food and sign. What deer are eating right now, and fresh sign and camera activity.
  6. Pressure. Stay out of your best spots until conditions line up.
  7. Moon and solunar periods as a final nudge between otherwise equal choices.

Try your own test

If you run trail cameras, you already have a data set. For each camera, note the time of each deer photo along with the moon phase and weather for that day. After a season or two, see whether movement on your ground shifts with the moon. Many hunters find that cold fronts and food sources show a much clearer pattern than the moon. Some find a mild moon pattern on a specific property. Either way, you'll be working from evidence, not a chart.

Some practical tips for a camera study:

  • Use the camera's timestamp, and make sure the clock is set correctly, including daylight saving time changes.
  • Separate daylight and night photos. Moving the needle on daylight movement is what matters for hunting.
  • Keep camera checks short and infrequent so you don't change deer behavior by checking.
  • Be careful drawing conclusions from small numbers. A dozen photos over a week isn't a trend.

Common moon myths, gently corrected

  • "Deer feed all night on a full moon, so mornings are dead." Some hunters report this, but studies haven't shown it consistently. Plenty of deer are killed in the morning after full moons.
  • "The rut is set by the hunter's moon." In most whitetail range, research points to day length as the main driver of breeding timing, with only a possible minor role for the moon.
  • "A 5-star solunar day guarantees deer." No table can guarantee movement. Weather and pressure can override it completely.

The bottom line

Use moon data honestly: as a light, timing and record-keeping tool, and maybe as a small factor when choosing between good days. Build the core of your plan on regulations, weather, wind, food, rut timing and pressure. And never skip a good-weather, good-wind day just because a table said the moon was wrong. You can't kill one from the couch.
