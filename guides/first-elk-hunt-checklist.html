The First-Time Elk Hunt Checklist

Fitness, scouting, tags, glassing, gear and meat care: a step-by-step checklist for planning your first elk hunt without the classic mistakes.

Home / Guides / First elk hunt checklist

Field School · High Country · about 5 min read · 1166 words

An elk hunt is one of the biggest steps most hunters ever take. The animals are big, the country is bigger, and the difference between a good trip and a miserable one usually comes down to preparation that happens months before opening day. This checklist walks through the decisions in the order you'll face them.

1. Decide what kind of elk hunt you're taking

"Elk hunting" covers very different trips:

  • Do-it-yourself public land: you plan, scout and hunt on your own, often on national forest or other public land. The cheapest option, and the most demanding.
  • Drop camp: an outfitter packs you, your camp and your gear in by horse or mule and comes back to pick you (and hopefully your elk) up. You hunt on your own.
  • Fully guided: a guide hunts with you, and the outfitter handles camp, food and often meat care.

Be honest about your fitness, experience, budget and the time you have. A guided or drop-camp hunt can be a smart first trip, and many hunters learn enough on one to go DIY later.

2. Understand licenses, draws and tags

Elk tags are handled very differently from state to state. Some states sell certain tags over the counter, others allocate most tags through draws with application deadlines months before the season, and many use preference or bonus point systems. Seasons are split by weapon (archery, muzzleloader, rifle) and often by unit.

Don't rely on memory, forums or last year's information. Go straight to the state wildlife agency's website for the current application dates, unit maps, season dates, tag types, legal weapons and any hunter education requirements. Once you have the official dates, put them into a calendar or app reminder so a deadline doesn't sneak past you.

3. Pick a unit and start e-scouting

Once you know where you can hunt, study the country from home:

  • Public land boundaries. Know exactly where public ground starts and stops, and where private land blocks access. Public-land map layers are a great planning tool, but they're informational. Confirm boundaries with official sources and on-the-ground signs.
  • Terrain. Look for north-facing timbered slopes (often used for bedding), benches, saddles, meadows and parks for feeding, and water such as springs, seeps and wallows.
  • Access. Trailheads, roads and their seasonal closures, and how far each spot is from the nearest road. Many hunters find that elk pressure drops off with distance and steepness.
  • Several plans. Mark a primary area and at least two backups. Other hunters, weather or a lack of elk may push you to move.

4. Train like the hunt depends on it

Fitness isn't optional in elk country. Steep slopes, thin air at elevation, and the possibility of packing out hundreds of pounds of meat make cardiovascular endurance and leg strength matter. Start months out:

  • Hike with a loaded pack on hills or stairs, gradually increasing weight and distance.
  • Break in your boots on those hikes so they're comfortable long before the trip.
  • If you're coming from low elevation, expect the altitude to slow you down for the first days. Hydrate, pace yourself, and know the signs of altitude illness.

Talk to your doctor before starting a demanding training program if you have any health concerns.

5. Build your shooting skills and set a range limit

Practice with the exact rifle, load, bow or arrows you'll hunt with. Practice from field positions: sitting, kneeling, off a pack or shooting sticks, and on slopes, not just from a bench. Decide on a maximum ethical range based on what you can do consistently on demand. That number is a personal limit, not a challenge to beat.

Know your effective range in wind too. Mountain winds swirl and can push shots more than you expect. Our shot distance guide covers the basics.

6. Gear: what actually matters

You can spend endlessly on elk gear. Focus first on the things that keep you safe, comfortable and able to stay out:

  • Boots: supportive and fully broken in.
  • Layers: a base layer, an insulating layer and a quiet rain shell. Mountain weather can go from warm sun to sleet in an afternoon.
  • Pack: one that fits your torso and can carry meat.
  • Optics: binoculars you can glass with for hours, and possibly a spotting scope and tripod for open country.
  • Calls: cow calls are useful throughout the season. Bugles are more situational.
  • Navigation and communication: a phone with offline maps downloaded, a backup battery, and a satellite messenger where there's no cell service. Carry a compass and know how to use it.
  • Sleep system rated for the coldest realistic night.
  • Meat care: game bags, a sharp knife and spare blades, cord and a plan for keeping meat cool.

Our Elk Backcountry Kit lays this out as a checklist.

7. Plan meat care before you need it

An elk is a lot of meat, and getting it out of the mountains and cooled quickly is one of the hunter's most important responsibilities. Before the trip, decide:

  • How you'll break the animal down (the gutless method is popular for elk) and how many trips the pack-out will take.
  • Where you'll hang meat in shade to cool, and how you'll protect it from flies and from bears where they're present.
  • How you'll keep it cold on the drive home: coolers, ice, or a processor near the hunt area.
  • What your state requires for evidence of sex, tagging, transport and, in some places, carcass movement rules related to chronic wasting disease. Check the agency's regulations before the trip.

8. Build the trip plan and share it

Write down where you'll park, where you'll camp, the areas you plan to hunt each day, and when you expect to be out. Share it with someone at home, along with a check-in schedule and what to do if you miss one. Include the nearest town, hospital and the county sheriff's or search-and-rescue contact.

9. Hunting strategy on the ground

  • Glass first. Get to a vantage point at first and last light and let your eyes do the walking.
  • Hunt the wind. Elk noses are as sharp as deer's. Thermals in steep country are strong and predictable. Morning air typically rises, evening air sinks.
  • Look for fresh sign: tracks, droppings, rubs and wallows. Old sign tells you where elk were, fresh sign where they are.
  • Be patient and flexible. If an area is empty, move. Elk can cover a lot of country in a day.

Final pre-trip checklist

  • License and tags in hand, carried the way the state requires.
  • Regulations for the unit printed or saved offline.
  • Rifle or bow zeroed and confirmed after travel.
  • Maps downloaded for offline use.
  • Trip plan shared and a check-in schedule set.
  • Meat care plan and gear packed.
  • Boots broken in, body trained, expectations realistic.

Plenty of first elk hunts end without an elk. If you come home having learned the country, with a plan for next year, the trip still counts.

Regulations: this guide is general information. Seasons, limits, licenses, legal methods, hours and access rules vary by state and country and change often. Check your state wildlife agency or the official authority before you hunt.
