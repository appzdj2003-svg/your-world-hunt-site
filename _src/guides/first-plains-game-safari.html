For many hunters, an African plains-game safari is a once-in-a-lifetime goal. Kudu in the thornveld, impala at dawn, the campfire talk at night. It's also a trip with more moving parts than most hunts: international travel, firearm paperwork, outfitter choice and a pricing structure that's different from hunting at home. This guide walks through the planning in a sensible order. Every country, outfitter and airline has its own rules, and those rules change. Treat everything about paperwork, permits and travel here as a list of things to confirm with your outfitter and official sources, not as instructions.

Step 1: Choose a country and type of hunt

Plains-game hunts are offered in several southern and eastern African countries. Destinations differ in species, terrain, climate, travel time and how hunts are run. Some hunts take place on large private properties, others on government or communal concessions. Things to consider:

  • Species you want to hunt and whether they're available where you're looking.
  • Hunting style. Walk-and-stalk, spot-and-stalk from vehicles, blinds at water, or a mix.
  • Season and climate. Many plains-game hunts in southern Africa take place during the dry winter months, when cover thins out and animals gather near water. Ask your outfitter about typical conditions for your dates.
  • Rifle or bow. Bowhunting safaris are popular and often run from blinds. Check that the country and the outfitter allow your chosen method.

Step 2: Choose an outfitter carefully

Your outfitter (sometimes called the professional hunter, or PH, and their company) shapes the entire experience. Take your time:

  • Ask for references, recent ones, from hunters who hunted similar species, and call them.
  • Ask about licensing. Confirm the outfitter and PH are licensed where they operate.
  • Meet them at a hunting convention or by video call. Ask many questions and see how clearly they answer.
  • Ask about the land. Size of the properties, whether they're fenced, how animals are managed, and what a typical day looks like.
  • Get everything in writing. The contract should list dates, daily rates, the species list and trophy fees, what's included, deposit and cancellation terms, and who handles which permits.

Step 3: Understand the pricing

Safari pricing is usually built from parts, and each outfitter structures it differently:

  • Daily rate: covers the hunter (and sometimes an observer at a different rate), usually including accommodation, meals and the guide.
  • Trophy fees: paid per animal taken, from the outfitter's price list. Ask how wounded-and-lost animals are handled. Many outfitters charge the trophy fee if an animal is wounded and not recovered.
  • Extras that may not be included: airport transfers, government taxes and levies, permits, firearm rental, ammunition, dipping and packing of trophies, shipping, tips, and alcohol.
  • Taxidermy and shipping can add significant cost and time, and import rules apply. Ask early.

Get the full price list from your outfitter and build a realistic budget. Include a buffer for tips and extras, and confirm customary tipping practices with your outfitter.

Step 4: Paperwork (confirm everything)

This is the part where details matter most and change most often. Work through it early, with your outfitter's guidance:

  • Passport with the validity and blank pages your destination requires.
  • Visa if required for your nationality.
  • Temporary firearm import permit for the destination country, if you bring your own rifle. The process, forms and timelines vary, and some outfitters use permit services. Many hunters choose to rent rifles from the outfitter to avoid this. Ask about the quality and availability of rental rifles.
  • Your home country's documentation for traveling with and bringing back firearms, which your customs agency may require.
  • Airline firearm policies for every carrier on your itinerary, including connections. Rules on cases, ammunition amounts and packaging, and declaring firearms differ, and some airlines don't carry firearms at all.
  • Trophy import rules for your home country. Some species have extra permit requirements.
  • Travel insurance that includes medical evacuation.
  • Health: talk to a travel clinic or doctor about vaccinations and malaria prevention for your destination well before you go.

Step 5: Shooting preparation

Most plains-game shots are taken off shooting sticks, a tripod or bipod the PH sets up for you, often standing, and often quickly. Many first-time safari hunters have never shot this way. Practice it:

  • Get a set of shooting sticks and practice standing shots at realistic distances.
  • Practice getting on target quickly. Animals may not stand for long.
  • Learn shot placement for the species you'll hunt. African animals' vital zones may differ from what you know at home. Study shot placement charts and ask your PH.
  • Know your realistic range off sticks and stick to it.
  • Zero your rifle with the exact ammunition you'll hunt with, then check zero again on arrival.

Caliber and bullet recommendations depend on the species and your outfitter's experience. Ask what they recommend, and check whether the destination has a minimum caliber or energy requirement for certain species.

Step 6: Packing

Outfitters typically do laundry often, so you can pack light. A common list:

  • Neutral-colored clothing, khaki, olive and brown. Ask your outfitter about camouflage; some countries restrict it.
  • A warm layer. Mornings and evenings in the southern African winter can be cold, especially on an open vehicle.
  • Quiet, broken-in boots.
  • Wide-brim hat, sunglasses, sunscreen and lip balm.
  • Binoculars, plus a rangefinder if your PH doesn't carry one.
  • A daypack, a headlamp and a camera.
  • Prescription medications in original containers, plus a basic first-aid kit.
  • Copies of passport, permits, insurance and the outfitter contract, stored separately from the originals.

Step 7: On safari

  • Listen to your PH. They know the animals, terrain and local rules. Their safety calls are final.
  • Be patient. Some species, like kudu, can take days to find. Enjoy the whole experience, not just the shot.
  • Ask questions about the land, conservation work and wildlife management in the area.
  • Respect the people and the culture. Trackers, skinners and camp staff are a big part of your hunt.

A planning timeline

  1. 12 to 18 months out: research countries and outfitters, get references, book and pay the deposit.
  2. 6 to 12 months out: book flights with firearm-friendly airlines, start permit paperwork, check your passport, start practicing off sticks.
  3. 3 to 6 months out: travel clinic visit, insurance, gear list, finalize the permit process with your outfitter.
  4. Final month: confirm all documents, make copies, confirm transfers, pack and weigh bags.

The single best piece of safari advice is to choose a trustworthy, well-reviewed outfitter and lean on their experience for every detail. Then go enjoy one of the great hunting adventures.
