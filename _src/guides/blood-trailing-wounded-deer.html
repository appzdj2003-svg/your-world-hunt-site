Every hunter who shoots will someday walk up to the spot where a deer stood and find a little blood, or none at all. What you do in the next few hours decides whether you recover that animal. Blood trailing is a skill: reading the sign, giving the deer the right amount of time, marking the trail so you never lose it, and searching methodically when it runs out. This guide covers the widely accepted basics taught in hunter and bowhunter education, plus the rules you need to check before you track with a dog or a light.

Right after the shot

The first minute matters more than the next hour. Before you do anything else:

  • Watch and listen. Note how the deer reacted. A deer hit in the vitals may jump or kick before running; a deer that hunches up and walks or wobbles off may be hit farther back. A crash in the woods often means a short trail.
  • Mark three places in your mind: where you were, where the deer stood, and the exact spot where you last saw it. Pick a landmark at each, like a split tree, a stump or a rock.
  • Take a bearing to the last-seen spot with a compass or your phone if it's out of sight.
  • Stay put. Unless the deer is down in sight, don't climb down and start walking toward it right away.

Assume you hit the animal unless you can prove otherwise. Many deer hit in the heart and lungs run hard as if they were missed.

Reading blood: what color and sign suggest

When it's time, go quietly to the spot where the deer stood and search for hair, blood, bone or tissue, and tracks. If you can find your arrow, look at it and smell it. Blood is a clue, not a diagnosis, but general patterns are taught in hunter education:

  • Bright pink or red with bubbles (frothy): usually a lung hit. Lots of frothy blood generally means a short trail.
  • Bright red, not frothy, often in sprays or heavy drops: can mean a heart or major artery hit.
  • Darker red: often a liver, kidney or muscle hit.
  • Dark or watery blood with green or brown bits of plant matter, or a foul smell on the arrow: a gut (stomach or intestine) hit.

Other clues help. Blood on both sides of the trail can mean the shot passed through. The height of blood smeared on brush hints at where the wound is. Little or no blood doesn't mean a miss: a high lung hit may barely bleed until the chest fills, and some hits just don't bleed much.

When to wait and when to go

Pushing a wounded deer out of its bed is the most common way to lose one. A deer that isn't pushed often lies down and dies close by; a deer that's jumped can travel a long way and leave little blood. Waiting times taught by bowhunter education and state agencies are guidelines, not exact rules:

  • Clear chest (heart or lung) hit: wait at least 20 to 30 minutes before following.
  • Unsure where you hit: wait 30 to 60 minutes, then start slowly and carefully.
  • Suspected liver hit: many experienced trackers wait several hours, often 3 to 6.
  • Gut hit: back out quietly and wait 6 hours or more. An evening gut hit often means coming back in the morning.

Weather, heat, rain washing out the trail and predators all pull you toward going sooner. Weigh them honestly, but rain alone isn't a good reason to push a deer that needs time. When in doubt, wait longer and bring help. If you have to back out, leave the way you came and mark your route.

Mark the trail, and always mark the last blood

A blood trail you can't find again is no trail at all. As you go:

  • Walk beside the trail, not on it, so you don't smear or bury sign you may need if you have to backtrack.
  • Mark every few spots with toilet paper or tissue (it breaks down) or flagging tape you collect afterward. Looking back along the marks shows the deer's direction of travel.
  • Never leave the last blood unmarked. Before you step past it, flag it so you can always return and restart from there.
  • Mark it digitally too. Mark the shot spot, the last-seen spot and each blood spot as a pin in Your World Hunt, so you have exact coordinates even after dark or if your flagging is hard to find.
  • Look up as well as down. Wounded deer don't always travel in a straight line. Check leaves and brush at body height and look ahead for the deer itself.

When the trail runs out: grid searching

Go back to the last blood and slow down. Then switch from following drops to searching ground methodically:

  1. Circle out. Starting at the last blood, walk in slowly widening circles or half-circles, checking the ground and brush closely before each step.
  2. Run a grid with help. With friends, stand in a line a few steps apart (closer in thick cover, farther in the open), mark both ends of the line, and sweep forward together so no strip is skipped. Then shift and sweep back.
  3. Check the likely places. Thick cover, downhill, creek bottoms and water, and the edges of bedding areas are where hurt deer often go.
  4. Use your eyes and ears. Crows, ravens, magpies and jays gathering can lead you to a dead animal.
  5. Stay ready. A deer can jump from cover. Know where your partners are, and only shoot if the deer and what's behind it are clear.

Tracking with light at night

Most recoveries after an evening hunt happen in the dark. A bright, steady light helps:

  • Carry a strong headlamp and a handheld flashlight, plus spare batteries. Some hunters find certain LED lights make blood stand out more; try yours on known blood before you need it.
  • Move slowly. At night, a missed drop is easy to walk past and hard to find again.
  • Mark your route and parking spot so you can get back out. GPS return in Your World Hunt can point you back to the truck with a bearing and distance.
  • Stop if it isn't safe. Getting lost or hurt helps nobody, and the trail will still be there at first light.

Check the law on lights and firearms at night. Some states restrict carrying a light with a firearm or bow after legal shooting hours, or limit the kind of light, and some ask you to contact a conservation officer first. Ask your state wildlife agency before you go out.

Tracking dogs: check your state's rules

A trained, leashed blood-tracking dog can find deer that people can't. Whether you can use one, and how, depends on where you hunt. Some states allow leashed tracking dogs with conditions, such as a permit for the handler, a maximum leash length, blaze orange, limits on carrying a firearm while tracking or rules for tracking after hours. Others don't allow it at all. Dogs don't give you permission to enter land you don't have permission to be on. Look up the current rules with your state wildlife agency, and find a tracker before the season so you have a number to call.

Ethics and the finish

  • Every hit is your responsibility. Make every reasonable effort to find a wounded animal. In some places a wounded animal that isn't recovered may count toward your bag limit.
  • Get permission before following a deer onto land you can't hunt, and follow your state's rules for that situation.
  • Approach a downed deer carefully, from behind and above if you can. If the eyes are open and touching near the eye gets no blink, it's likely dead. Be ready for a finishing shot if it isn't.
  • Tag and report exactly as your state requires before you move or field dress it.
  • Learn from every trail. Note what you saw, how long you waited and where the deer went. It sharpens your shot choices too.
