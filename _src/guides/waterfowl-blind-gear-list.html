Waterfowl hunting is a gear-heavy sport. You're often in cold water, before dawn, in wind and rain, trying to hide from birds with excellent eyesight. Bring too little and you're miserable or unsafe. Bring too much and you're hauling a sled of gear across a marsh in the dark. This list covers what really matters in a duck or goose blind, grouped by job, with notes on why each item earns its place.

Before the gear: rules and stamps

Migratory bird hunting in the United States involves both federal and state requirements, typically a state license plus federal and often state waterfowl stamps or permits, and registration in the Harvest Information Program (HIP). Seasons, daily bag limits by species, shooting hours and zones are set each year, and shot-type rules apply (non-toxic shot is required for waterfowl nationwide in the US). Check your state wildlife agency's current waterfowl regulations before every season. The details change, and species limits can be complicated.

Staying dry and warm

  • Chest waders. Insulated waders for cold water, lighter breathable ones for early season. Always wear a wading belt cinched tight. It helps keep water out if you fall.
  • Layers under the waders. A wicking base layer, a fleece or wool mid-layer, and warm socks (bring spares in a dry bag). Avoid cotton.
  • Waterproof jacket. A wading-length jacket that's waterproof and has room for layers. Hand-warmer pockets help.
  • Gloves. Waterproof decoy gloves for setting spreads, plus thinner gloves for shooting and calling.
  • Hat and face cover. A warm hat and a face mask or face paint.
  • Hand warmers. Small, cheap and a real morale booster on frozen mornings.

Concealment

Ducks and geese can spot a shiny face, an unnatural shape or a flash of movement from far away. Concealment is often the difference between birds finishing and birds flaring.

  • Face mask or paint. A bare face looking up at circling birds is a classic way to flare them.
  • Camouflage matching the cover: marsh grass, timber, or field stubble patterns.
  • Blind: a boat blind, a layout blind for fields, a permanent blind, or simply natural cover. Whatever you use, brush it in with local vegetation so it matches its surroundings. Check rules before cutting vegetation on public land.
  • Stay still until it's time to shoot. Movement gets more hunts busted than color.

Decoys and spreads

  • Decoys that match the species you hunt and look realistic in the light you hunt in. Quantity depends on the setting. A small creek may need only a handful, while a big open-water or field spread may need far more.
  • Weights and line sized for the water depth and current. Tangles in the dark are a time thief, so wrap them properly.
  • Decoy bag with shoulder straps for carrying in.
  • Motion: jerk strings or other motion devices can make a spread look alive on still days. Some states restrict spinning-wing decoys during certain periods or seasons. Check your regulations.
  • Spread layout: leave a clear landing hole within your effective range, and set up so birds land into the wind toward you. Ducks and geese land into the wind, so the wind should generally be at your back or crossing.

Calls

  • Duck call: a single-reed is easier to learn, a double-reed is more forgiving and raspier.
  • Goose call if you hunt geese. Short-reed calls are versatile but take practice.
  • Lanyard to keep calls handy and out of the water.
  • Practice before the season. The most common calling mistake is calling too much, especially when birds are already working.

Shotgun and shells

Bring your shotgun with a plug if your regulations limit magazine capacity for migratory birds, plus non-toxic shells suited to the birds and distances you'll hunt. Pattern your gun with your chosen load at realistic distances before the season. A good pattern at a reasonable range beats long shots, and waterfowl ranges are easy to overestimate. Carry shells in a waterproof container or blind bag, and keep the action clean and dry.

The blind bag

A waterproof blind bag keeps the small stuff organized and dry:

  • License, stamps and permits as required.
  • Shells.
  • Calls and lanyard.
  • Headlamp and spare batteries.
  • Knife and multi-tool.
  • Choke tube wrench and spare choke.
  • Hand warmers, snacks and a thermos.
  • Phone in a waterproof case.
  • A small first-aid kit.
  • Bird strap or game bag.
  • Toilet paper. You'll thank yourself.

Safety in cold water

Cold water is the real danger in waterfowl hunting. Falls from boats and in deep water can quickly become life-threatening.

  • Wear a life jacket (personal flotation device) in the boat, every time. Inflatable models designed for hunters are less bulky.
  • Don't overload the boat. Follow the capacity plate, and remember decoys, dogs and gear add up fast.
  • Check the weather and wind before launching. Big water can become dangerous fast in high wind.
  • Know the ice. Late-season ice can be unpredictable. Never trust it without proper knowledge and safety gear.
  • Tell someone your plan, including where you launch and when you'll be back.
  • Carry a way to call for help and a change of dry clothes in the truck.
  • Muzzle discipline in a crowded blind is critical. Agree on who shoots where before birds arrive.

Dog gear

If you hunt with a retriever:

  • Neoprene vest for warmth in cold water.
  • Dog stand or platform to keep your dog out of the water between retrieves.
  • Whistle and lead.
  • Dog first-aid kit, plus fresh water and a towel.
  • Awareness of the cold. Watch for signs of hypothermia and fatigue, and talk with your vet about cold-weather care.

Packing list summary

  1. License, stamps, HIP registration and current regulations checked.
  2. Waders, wading belt, layers, jacket, gloves, hat and face cover.
  3. Decoys, weights, decoy bag and motion device if legal.
  4. Calls and lanyard.
  5. Shotgun with plug if required, and non-toxic shells.
  6. Blind bag with the small essentials.
  7. Life jacket, headlamp, phone in a dry case, first aid.
  8. Dog gear.

Get the warm, dry and safe parts right first. A hunter who's comfortable stays still, stays out longer, and makes better decisions when the birds finally commit.
